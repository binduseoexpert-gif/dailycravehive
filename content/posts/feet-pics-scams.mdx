## The Most Common Feet Pic Scams

### 1. The Fake Payment Screenshot

**How it works:** The buyer says they've paid and sends a screenshot, a forwarded "receipt" or an email that looks like it came from a payment app. They ask you to send the content now.

**Why it works:** Screenshots and emails are easy to fake. A real payment shows up in your own account, not in an image someone sends you.

**What to do:** Open your payment app or platform yourself, without clicking any link the buyer sent, and check the balance. The FTC's guidance on [mobile payment apps](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/mobile-payment-apps-how-avoid-scam-when-you-use-one) says scammers target these apps because "once you do [send money], it's hard for you to get your money back." No payment in your account means no content.

### 2. The Overpayment And Refund

**How it works:** The buyer "accidentally" pays you too much, often by check or a payment that can later be reversed. They ask you to send back the difference.

**What the FTC says:** Its [fake check scam guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-fake-check-scams) describes buyers who "'accidentally' send a check for too much, and ask you to refund the balance. But that's a scam." The FTC's guide on refund and recovery scams adds: "It can take weeks for a bank to discover that a check it cleared was a fake. In the meantime, if you return money to the scammer, the bank will want you to repay that money."

**What to do:** Don't refund an overpayment yourself. If it happened on a platform, contact the platform. If it happened privately, don't touch the funds until your bank confirms the payment is final.

### 3. The Weekly Allowance Or "Sugar" Offer

**How it works:** A "generous" buyer offers a regular allowance, often hundreds of dollars a week, just for pictures or attention. There's a catch. Before the first payment can arrive, you need to pay something: an "activation fee," a "transfer fee," a "verification" payment, or a small amount to "prove you're not a scammer."

**Why it works:** The promised income is big enough that a small fee seems worth it.

**What to do:** Any request to pay money to receive money is a scam. The FTC's refund and recovery scams guidance makes the same point: anyone who asks for upfront fees to send you money "is a scammer." Real buyers pay you. You never pay them.

### 4. Gift Card Payments

**How it works:** The buyer offers to pay in gift cards, or asks you to buy a gift card to "unlock" a payment or cover a fee.

**What the FTC says:** "Gift cards are for gifts, not payments. Anyone who demands payment by gift card is always a scammer," according to the FTC's fake check scam guide. Its gift card scam guide says: "Only scammers will tell you to buy a gift card... and give them the numbers off the back of the card."

**What to do:** Don't accept gift cards as payment and never buy one for a buyer.

### 5. The Free "Verification" Photo

**How it works:** Before they'll pay, the buyer wants a free custom photo, such as your foot with their name written next to it, "to prove you're real."

**Why it works:** It sounds reasonable. But that custom photo is the product, and once you've sent it, they have no reason to pay.

**What to do:** Point them to your watermarked profile previews. If a buyer needs proof you're real, a verified marketplace profile is enough.

### 6. "Kindly" Messages And Scripted Openers

**How it works:** Sellers commonly report messages with the same scripted tone: overly formal or overly familiar wording, phrases like "kindly send," fast compliments and pressure to act now. These aren't proof of a scam on their own. Plenty of genuine buyers write in a second language. But together with pressure and an unusual payment request, they're a warning sign.

**What to do:** Judge buyers by what they ask you to do, not how they write. Follow the payment rules every time.

### 7. Pressure To Move Off-Platform

**How it works:** The buyer wants to move straight to another messaging or payment app. Once you're off the marketplace, there's no record and no protection.

**What to do:** Keep agreeing the order, payment and delivery on the platform, especially with new buyers. If a platform's rules forbid off-site payment, moving off-site can also risk your account.

### 8. Sextortion And Doxxing Threats

**How it works:** Someone gets your images or personal details and threatens to share them with your family, employer or followers unless you pay.

**What the FBI says:** The FBI's guidance on [financially motivated sextortion](https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/the-financially-motivated-sextortion-threat) is mainly aimed at minors and young adults, but its advice applies widely: get help "before sending money or more images," because "cooperating with the predator rarely stops the blackmail." It also advises reporting the account through the platform's safety feature, blocking the person and saving every interaction.

**What to do:** Don't pay. Save the messages, report the account, block it and contact law enforcement. In the US, the FBI takes reports at tips.fbi.gov.

## Red Flags Checklist Before You Accept A Custom Order

Before you take a custom order, run through this list. If you tick even one, slow down:

- The buyer wants content **before** a confirmed payment shows in your account
- They send payment "proof" as a screenshot or link
- They paid too much and want money back
- They offer an allowance and need a fee first
- They mention gift cards, wire transfers or crypto
- They want a free custom "verification" photo
- They push to move off the platform immediately
- They ask for your real name, location, workplace or other social accounts
- They create urgency: "right now," "last chance," "my account closes today"

## What To Do If You've Been Scammed

Acting quickly gives you the best chance of recovering money. The FTC's guide [What To Do if You Were Scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed) suggests these steps:

1. **Payment app:** report it to the app immediately and ask them to reverse the payment.
2. **Gift card:** contact the gift card company right away and ask for your money back. Keep the card and receipt.
3. **Wire transfer:** contact the wire company or bank, report a fraudulent transfer and ask for a reversal.
4. **Report the scam:** in the US, report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
5. **Report the account** to the platform where you met the buyer.
6. **Threats or blackmail:** save everything and contact law enforcement.

Outside the US, contact your local police and your country's consumer protection or cybercrime agency. Rules vary by country.
